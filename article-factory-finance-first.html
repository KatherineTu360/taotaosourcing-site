Chinese Factory Due Diligence: What Observable Risk Signals Can—and Cannot—Tell You

By Katherine Tu · Published September 4, 2026 · Updated September 12, 2026 · 7 min read

Before working in sourcing, I spent more than ten years at a state-owned bank, including corporate lending work involving manufacturing businesses. That experience taught me to separate a company's presentation from the evidence available for a specific decision.

Product quality is only one part of order risk. The supplier's legal identity, operating role, production controls, commercial terms and communication can also affect the order. Observable signals can identify questions that need further verification, but they cannot predict performance on their own.

The approach below uses public records, supplier-provided documents and observable factory-side evidence. It does not rely on non-public bank data and does not determine a supplier's actual solvency or creditworthiness.

Signal 1: How payment terms allocate transaction risk

Payment terms show how a transaction's risks and cash requirements are allocated; they do not diagnose a factory's cash flow. Do not treat 30/70 terms, 100% advance payment, a letter of credit or open account as stand-alone proof of either financial health or distress. Assess each payment milestone against the order value, tooling and customization, the trading relationship, the evidence available before payment and the banking options open to both parties. If the beneficiary or bank details change, pause and verify the request through contact information obtained independently of the change request.

Signal 2: Whether quotations are genuinely comparable

Before judging a price difference, normalize the specification, material, included components, quantity, tooling, testing, packaging, Incoterms® rule, named place and edition, payment terms, lead time and remedy. A quotation below the other offers is not proof of corner-cutting or financial distress; it is a variance that needs an explanation. Ask the supplier to identify every deviation and exclusion, then check whether the difference comes from material, process, capacity, existing tooling, commercial strategy or a quotation error.

Signal 3: Whether factory-side evidence matches earlier claims

Use an agreed live walkthrough or on-site review to observe the stated location, relevant equipment, production processes and approximate staffing at a defined time. Compare those observations with earlier answers and available records. Treat the result as dated evidence only: one visit cannot establish equipment ownership, long-term staffing stability, undisclosed subcontracting or future capacity without corroboration.

Signal 4: Whether a price change has a clear commercial basis

A fast discount is not evidence of financial distress or future quality failure. Ask what changed: specification, quantity, material, process, lead time, payment, packaging, capacity or margin. Record every resulting deviation in the quotation and purchase documents. A price change without a clear explanation is a reason for further clarification, not a verdict on the supplier.

Signal 5: Whether capability claims are supported

Claims of unlimited capability, immediate lead times or broad certification coverage should be tested against model-specific documents and observable production evidence. A supplier that states its limits clearly gives the buyer a more usable basis for planning, but candor alone does not prove capability, integrity or future performance.

What this review cannot determine: solvency, creditworthiness, beneficial ownership, future delivery performance or product compliance. Its purpose is to identify evidence gaps and decide what additional checks are proportionate. Depending on the order, those checks may include official registration records, document verification, an authorized on-site review, agreed product inspection, qualified laboratory testing or professional legal and customs advice.

Review the supplier and the product together

Some product problems can be detected or corrected before shipment; others—especially safety, regulatory or intellectual-property failures—may be serious and difficult to remedy. Supplier risk and product risk should therefore be reviewed together before payment and again before shipment. No screening method can guarantee delivery, product conformity or recovery of funds.
